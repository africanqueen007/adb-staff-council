# The Fine Print Club: Series Concept

**A video series that explains ADB policy changes to staff, hosted by the Chair of the ADB Staff Council**

Draft concept, 8 November 2026

---

## 1. The Idea in One Line

From the day staff join ADB, they are automatically members of the ADB Staff Association.[^sa] In this series, they find out they belong to a second club too: **The Fine Print Club**. Its Chair (you) reads every page of every policy change so nobody else has to, then explains in plain language what changed, who it affects, and what to do.

> **Catchphrase:** "Pull up a chair."
> **Sign-off:** "But what do I know. I only read the whole thing."

The joke works because it is true. Policies are not secret. They are long and full of acronyms, and most people never get past page two. The Chair does.

### 2.3 The Anatomy of a "Secrets Club" Video

I read the auto-generated transcripts of three of his Secrets Club videos ("Costco secret menu," "Raising Canes Sauce Has Lemon," and "Fast food worker found out where I lived").[^yt] All three follow the same five beats:

1. **The summons.** Someone needs him. *"It seems as though I have been summoned again."* Or a restaurant manager emails him, or a fast-food worker messages him.
2. **"Come here."** He leans in. Now it is just you and him.
3. **The escalating mini-story.** He tells you what happened in deadpan reported speech: *"and I said… and they said…"* (For example, the Costco staff forget to put him on hold and he hears them laughing.)
4. **The payload.** A fast, exact list: *"3 cups of warm water, 3 tbsp of yeast, 9 cups of flour…"* This is the useful part, the part people save and share.
5. **The button.** A small, deadpan line to close: *"But like I said, what do I know."* Or simply *"Enjoy."*

### 2.3a What the Camera Does (Scene Analysis)

The transcripts miss the visuals, so I ran "Raising Canes Sauce Has Lemon" (1:19) through a scene-by-scene video analysis tool.[^scene] The camera work carries the structure:

| Time | Shot | What it adds |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:12 | Medium close-up, phone selfie in a brightly lit bathroom mirror. He holds up a small brown leather notebook and leans toward the lens on "Come here." | The ritual: same place, same prop, every time |
| 0:12–0:51 | Tighter close-up while he tells the story. **He says "Come here" a second time** at about 0:29. | The camera moves closer as the story builds. The second "Come here" pulls drifting viewers back in. |
| 0:51–1:03 | Lower angle. **Gold-rimmed glasses on**, reading the measurements from the open notebook. | A visual cue that the useful part has started |
| 1:03–1:08 | Top-down shots of the ingredients being mixed: "Here's all the ingredients before I mix them… here's what it looks like after." | **Proof.** He does not just say it; he shows it. |
| 1:08–1:13 | **Side-by-side:** his homemade sauce next to the restaurant's real one | The payoff. Viewers can judge for themselves. |
| 1:13–1:19 | He tastes it at the kitchen counter, looks at the camera with a small, knowing smile: "But like I said, what do I know?" | The deadpan close |

Two things I had missed from the transcript alone: **the proof beat** (he shows the thing next to the real one) and **the second "Come here"** used as a mid-video re-hook.

### 2.4 The Shorts Formula

His recent Shorts follow a tighter pattern:[^yt]

- **Opener:** *"Do not worry, I found it."*
- **Reveal:** shows the thing, with real enthusiasm (*"Oh my goodness"*).
- **Deadpan tag:** *"Update: turns out you can't buy anything in the inconvenience store… Also, I lost my boat."*

The scene analysis of "Inconvenience store" (19 seconds) shows how little it takes.[^scene] It is one handheld phone, natural light, with no set and no edit tricks. There is a close-up of him on a boat, then a wide shot of the floating store, then **a cut to him walking down a city street at night** for the "Update." The change of place and light tells you time has passed, so the deadpan tag lands without any explanation.

### 2.5 The Long-Form Formula (Closest to Your 5–10 Minutes)

His 8-minute video "We don't have a name for the show yet" is a mockumentary about a cooking show going wrong.[^yt] What carries it is not the cooking. It is the crew talking back (*"Do we get producer money?"*), his honest self-mockery (*"sometimes you go through imposter syndrome"*), and the gap between how confident he acts and how things are going.

The scene analysis of this video shows *how* it holds attention for 8 minutes:[^scene]

- **Fast cutting.** The tool found 81 separate shots in about 7 minutes 57 seconds. That is 477 seconds ÷ 81 shots ≈ **one new shot every 6 seconds**.
- **Short B-roll inserts.** Shots of 1–3 seconds (ingredients, a sizzling pan, water pouring) between talking moments, over a light acoustic music bed. They give the eye something new without stopping the story.
- **Costume versus competence.** He wears a full white chef's coat and beanie while clearly not knowing what he is doing. The joke is the gap between the costume and the skill.
- **A behind-the-scenes cold open.** It starts with "We're rolling!" and a quick montage before he walks in.
- **The crew stays off camera until the climax.** They are heard for six minutes and only appear when they physically step in to help drain the pasta. That makes their entrance a payoff.
- **A polished hero shot after the chaos.** A beautifully plated dish, lit softly, right after the mess. The contrast is the punchline.
- **An abrupt exit.** "This isn't even my house. I'm leaving." The crew are left to clean up, then fade to black.

Short videos reach far more people on his channel than long ones (YouTube public view counts, observed 8 November 2026):[^yt]

What this means for you: keep the 5–10 minute episodes, but cut each one into two or three short vertical clips that send people to the full episode. Your audience is different (ADB staff, not the open internet), so treat this as a pattern, not a prediction.

## 3. Translating His Formula to Your Context

**One important change.** His "secrets" are things companies supposedly do not want you to know. If a Staff Council chair framed ADB policies that way, it would suggest Management hides things, which is unfair and would cost you credibility on both sides. So the premise moves from *leaked secrets* to *buried in plain sight*. The enemy is the fine print, never the people.

| His move | Why it works | Your version |
|---|---|---|
| "Come here" | Intimacy; you are an insider now | **"Pull up a chair."** You roll into frame on an office chair. It is a pun on your title, and it is your visual signature. |
| Reading glasses + leather-bound recipe book | A ritual that says "the good part is coming" | Reading glasses + **The Binder**: a thick binder of policy text with four colored tabs in the Staff Association logo colors (see Section 5) |
| President of the Fast Food Secrets Club | Belonging to a club | **Chair of The Fine Print Club.** Every staff member is already a member, just as they are already members of the Staff Association.[^sa] |
| The summons | A real person's problem pulls you in | **The Summons:** each episode opens with a real (anonymized, with permission) question from a staff member |
| Exact, rapid-fire recipe | Pure usefulness; people save it | **The Recipe:** exact dates, steps, eligibility, and the policy reference, on screen |
| "If you don't believe me, I'll make it for you" + side-by-side with the real sauce | Proof builds trust; viewers check it themselves | **The Receipt:** "If you don't believe me, here's page [x]." Show the actual policy paragraph on screen **side by side** with your plain-language version. This is the strongest credibility device in the whole series. |
| A second "Come here" mid-video | Re-hooks viewers who are drifting | A second **"Pull up a chair"** just before the field trip |
| "Does that make sense?" | Checks understanding without talking down | **"Still with me?"** at each segment change |
| "Update:" tag / "what do I know" | A comic release that makes it memorable | **"Update:"** end card + *"But what do I know. I only read the whole thing."* |
| Crew talking back | Mockumentary texture for longer videos | **The Off-Camera Colleague:** asks the question staff are thinking ("So… does this affect me?") |
| Celebrity cameos | Surprise | **Colleague cameos** from across ADB, and occasionally a senior manager delivering one deadpan line (only if they agree) |
| Greets the janitor | Sincerity builds trust | You speak for all staff, so feature all staff: drivers, security, cafeteria teams, and resident mission colleagues |
| One take, phone, same mirror | Cheap, repeatable, authentic | Phone, one clip-on mic, the same corner of the Staff Council office every time |

**Borrow the mechanics, not the man.** Do not copy his catchphrases, his look, or his name, and do not imply any link to him. The table above gives you your own versions of each device.

## 4. The Character: You, as "The Chair"

Jordan's persona works because it is an amplified version of himself: "It really is me."[^upworthy] Yours should be too. Pick the tone that is closest to how you already speak at town halls:

| Tone | What it looks like | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **The Calm Insider** (closest to Jordan) | Low voice, leans in, slow and sincere. Treats a pension rule like classified intelligence. | Building trust; sensitive topics |
| **The Weary Translator** | Has read too many Administrative Orders. Deadpan exasperation at jargon, never at people. | Acronym-heavy, procedural changes |
| **The Over-Prepared Superfan** | Loves the binder a little too much. Label maker. Color-coded everything. | Lighter topics; recurring gags |

**My recommendation:** start as the Calm Insider, with one Over-Prepared prop gag (the binder that gets thicker every episode). A calm host makes the jokes land and keeps your credibility as the elected voice of staff.

## 5. Episode Format (About 7 Minutes)

The four binder tabs use the four colors in the ADB Staff Association logo. Each color is one segment, so viewers always know where they are.

| Time | Segment | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:40 | **Cold open: The Summons** | You read a real staff question on your phone. Beat. "Pull up a chair." |
| 0:40–0:50 | **Title sting** | Binder lands on the desk. *The Fine Print Club.* |
| 0:50–2:00 | 🟧 **Orange tab: What changed** | The one-sentence version first. Then a before/after card. |
| 2:00–3:00 | 🟨 **Yellow tab: Who it affects** | Which staff, where (headquarters, resident missions), and from when. "Still with me?" |
| 3:00–5:00 | **Field trip** | Open with a second "Pull up a chair" to re-hook. Then the mockumentary part: you try to actually do the thing (fill in the form, find the page, ask the help desk). The Off-Camera Colleague comments. Colleagues cameo. Change location and light so the field trip feels like a different place. |
| 5:00–6:00 | 🟩 **Green tab: The Recipe + The Receipt** | Reading glasses on. Rapid-fire, exact steps and dates on screen. Then: "If you don't believe me, here's page [x]," with the actual policy paragraph shown side by side with your plain-language version. |
| 6:00–6:40 | 🟦 **Blue tab: Where the Council stands** | What the Council raised, what is settled, what is still open, and how staff can give input. Plain and factual. |
| 6:40–7:00 | **Button** | Glasses off. "But what do I know. I only read the whole thing." Roll out of frame. **"Update:"** card. Call to action: send a question, and it may become the next Summons. |

**Pacing and look (from the scene analysis of his long-form video):**

- **Cut often.** Aim for a new shot roughly every 6 seconds. Fill the gaps with 1–3 second inserts: a stamp hitting a form, a cursor on the intranet, the binder tabs, the building lobby.
- **Dress for the role you are bad at.** In field-trip segments, wear a costume for the job you are trying out ("help desk officer for a day," with an oversized lanyard). The gap between costume and competence is the joke. The joke is always on you.
- **The hero shot.** After the field-trip chaos, cut to one clean, softly lit shot of the finished result: the completed form, or a one-page summary card. That is your plated dish.
- **Keep the Off-Camera Colleague off camera** until the one moment they have to step in and help you. Then it is a payoff.
- **Exit abruptly.** Roll out of frame and leave the colleague holding the binder.

**Short cut-downs (two or three per episode, 30–60 seconds, vertical):**

- **"Relax. I found it."** The Recipe segment alone, opening on the page number.
- **The funniest field-trip moment.**
- **The Summons as a teaser**, ending with where to watch the full episode.

## 6. Recurring Bits

- **The Acronym Jar.** Anyone who uses an unexplained acronym on camera drops a coin in the jar, including you.
- **The Growing Binder.** One more tab every episode. By the end of the season, it does not close.
- **The Off-Camera Colleague.** Never seen, always skeptical. Asks what the audience is thinking.
- **Mission Mail.** A 10-second selfie question from a colleague at a resident mission, so the series is not headquarters-only.
- **The Cameo.** A colleague from anywhere in ADB delivers one line. Occasionally a senior manager, only with their agreement.
- **The "Update:" card.** Every episode ends with a deadpan postscript about something that happened during filming.

## 8. Guardrails

These matter more for you than for Jordan, because you are an elected representative and ADB staff are your only audience.

1. **The Recipe must be exactly right.** Every fact in the Green-tab segment should be checked against the official policy text, ideally by the policy owner. Show the policy reference on screen. Comedy can bend; the facts cannot.
2. **Punch at the paperwork, never at people.** Jokes target jargon, length, acronyms, and your own confusion. Never colleagues, named managers, departments, or nationalities.
3. **Make humor that travels.** ADB has 69 members, 50 of them from Asia and the Pacific.[^about] For many staff, English is not a first language. Favor visual and deadpan humor over wordplay, and always add captions.
4. **Keep "Where the Council stands" separate.** If the Council disagrees with a change, say so plainly in the Blue-tab segment. Do not let a joke blur what the policy says versus what the Council thinks.
5. **Turn down the comedy for heavy topics.** For topics such as separation, misconduct, or grievance procedures, keep the format and the warmth, but cut most of the gags.
6. **Ask before you film people.** Get consent from every colleague on camera, and anonymize staff questions unless the sender agrees to be named.
7. **Check the approval path first.** Find out what ADB's internal communications rules require before staff-wide release. I do not know what those rules are.

## 9. Production Plan (Low Budget, Repeatable)

**Kit:** a phone, one clip-on microphone, one light, a rolling office chair, and The Binder.

**Team (volunteers from the Council are enough):**

- **Host:** you
- **Writer-producer:** drafts the script with you
- **Camera / Off-Camera Colleague:** one person can do both
- **Fact-checker:** the policy owner or the Council's policy lead
- **Editor:** cuts the episode and the Shorts

**Workflow per episode:**

1. Pick the change and gather real staff questions (the Summons).
2. Write the Recipe segment first and have it fact-checked. Write the jokes around it.
3. Film in one session, in as few takes as you can, as Jordan does.
4. Edit, add captions and on-screen text, and cut two or three Shorts.
5. Release the full episode and the Shorts on ADB's internal channels.

**Tools in this workspace:** Descript (edit by transcript, captions, short clips) and Canva (thumbnails and end cards) are connected here. Once you have footage, I can help with both.

## 10. How to Tell Whether It Works

- Views as a share of staff, and how many viewers watch to the end
- Number of questions sent in for future Summons
- A one-question pulse poll after each episode: "Do you understand what changed?"
- Fewer repeat questions to the Staff Council inbox about the same change

## 11. Decisions for You

1. **Title.** Recommended: *The Fine Print Club*. Alternatives: *Pull Up a Chair*, *Read It So You Don't Have To*, *Still With Me?*
2. **Tone.** Calm Insider, Weary Translator, or Over-Prepared Superfan (Section 4).
3. **Approval path.** Who needs to see episodes before they go out.
4. **First real policy.** Which change to cover after the pilot.
